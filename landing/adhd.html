Task apps, GTD, and ADHD

Most people arrive at a page like this with a graveyard behind them: two or three apps, a bullet journal, a whiteboard, and a system that worked beautifully for eleven days. This page is about task-manager design, not medical advice — what makes a system survivable when attention and working memory are unreliable, and what that asks of the software.

The system asked for too much.

When a productivity system gets abandoned, the usual reading is that the person lacked discipline. The more accurate reading is that the system was charging more than it paid out. Every system has an upkeep cost — tags to maintain, priorities to re-score, a planning ritual every morning, a hierarchy to keep tidy. When the cost lands up front and the benefit arrives later, the whole thing is fragile for everyone. It is the first thing to go when a week gets loud.

The second cost is quieter. After a lapse, the list reads as a record of what you didn’t do: forty overdue items in red, each one an accusation. Re-opening it is unpleasant enough that you don’t, and the gap widens. Plenty of systems are pleasant to start and punishing to return to. That is a flaw in the design, not in you.

What makes a system survivable

Whatever tool you use — paper, plain text, an app — these four properties tend to separate the systems that last from the ones in the graveyard.

  • Capture faster than the thought evaporates

    An idea has a shelf life measured in seconds. If writing it down takes unlocking, launching, navigating, and choosing a project first, it doesn’t get written down — and a system that isn’t fed stops being worth opening.

  • One next step, not the whole mountain

    A project shown in full is a wall. Ask for the single physical action rather than the state of the whole kitchen, and keep everything else out of sight while you answer.

  • External memory, because held memory runs out

    Even under careful laboratory conditions, estimates of how much a person can actively hold in mind land around four items (Cowan, 2001). Nobody has enough. The fix is to stop using memory for storage.

  • Structure that survives a bad week

    You will lapse. Assume it, and judge a system by how cheap it is to re-enter. If picking it back up costs an evening of cleanup and a wall of red, the lapse becomes permanent.

What an app can’t do.

No software makes executive function reliable. A task manager can lower the cost of a handful of specific actions — writing something down, finding the next step, remembering at the right moment. It cannot supply the start, and anyone promising otherwise is selling something.

So take the parts that help and leave the rest. On some days an index card with three lines on it will beat any app ever written, and that is a good outcome. You also don’t need the whole method: an Inbox you trust and a list of genuine next actions is already a working system, and the rest of GTD can wait indefinitely. And nothing on this page is advice about ADHD itself — that’s a conversation for people who know your situation.
